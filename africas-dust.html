The real story

It began with a disaster

In the summer of 1941 a young officer named David Stirling talked his way into raising a small unit to attack airfields far behind enemy lines. It was called L Detachment, Special Air Service Brigade. There was no brigade. The name was a bluff, meant to make the enemy think a whole airborne force had arrived in Egypt.

Their first raid, in November 1941, went in by parachute, at night, into a storm. Around fifty-five men jumped. Twenty-one came back. Not one enemy aircraft was touched.

Most units would have ended there. Instead they changed the plan. The Long Range Desert Group, who knew the deep desert better than anyone, would drive them in and bring them out. A few weeks later they tried again.

This time it worked. In a fortnight in December 1941, a few dozen men destroyed dozens of aircraft on the ground, more than many fighter squadrons managed in the air. Africa's Dust starts there.

How it plays

Get in quietly. Get everyone out.

Normandy was open battle. This is something else. You have a few men, the dark, and a bag of bombs, and the airfield has sentries, guns and far more soldiers than you do.

The raids

Real places, real nights

  • December 1941

    Tamet

    The raid that proved it could be done. A small party walks onto a coastal airfield and leaves it burning.

  • July 1942

    Bagush

    The bombs fail. So they drive back onto the airfield in jeeps and finish the job with machine guns. A new way of raiding is born by accident.

  • 1942

    The coast road

    One road carries everything the enemy needs. Fuel, food and ammunition all travel it by night.

  • 26 July 1942

    Sidi Haneish

    Eighteen jeeps in formation, straight down the runway, every gun firing. The largest raid of them all.

The men

The only thing that matters is who comes back
